My (currently) Philosophical Ideas, Poorly Summarized:

My philosophical stance emerges from the convergence of fallibilism, scientific liberal pragmatism, a relational coherentist framework, and an evolutionary darwinian understanding of history and politics. I do not seek ultimate foundations or absolute truths. What matters is justification: the operational coherence of conceptual systems, their empirical responsiveness, and their capacity to sustain, revise, and transform themselves within complex and changing informational environments.

Truth, in this sense, is not correspondence with an independent metaphysical essence of the world, but a functional property of conceptual networks: it is the long-term stability of models that successfully constrain expectations, generate reliable predictions, and remain resilient under empirical pressure. Reality is not accessed directly, but through the structural constraints it imposes on our descriptive and explanatory frameworks: models persist insofar as they succeed, fail, or are corrected in interaction with the world. Accordingly, truth is a function of the stability and integrative capacity of conceptual networks over time, while reality is that which, structurally, constrains, differentiates, and stabilizes patterns within coherent informational frameworks, where logic, statistics, physics, and, more broadly, a solid scientific naturalism prevail.

I have been deeply influenced by Peirce, Carnap, Frank Ramsey, Gerhard Vollmer, Ludwig Boltzmann, Dretske, Reichenbach, Shannon, and Wolpert (among many others). I adopt a deflationary and structural approach to philosophical inquiry. Objects, identities, and persistence are not treated as primitive givens, but as functional roles within systems of relations. For this reason I privilege formal tools such as topology, model and category theory, which allow identity, continuity, and transformation to be described in terms of structural relations, mappings, and invariants, rather than through intrinsic properties. The world is thus approached as a dynamic network of relations, describable through informational structures, hierarchical models, or conditional configurations, never as a mere collection of isolated entities.

Within this framework, history is not a linear or teleological process, nor the unfolding of moral ideals, but an emergent trajectory shaped by technological, institutional, and ecological variation. Change occurs through selective retention: tools, languages, and institutions persist when they prove more adaptive within their informational and environmental constraints. Societies do not evolve through moral enlightenment alone or through collective will, but because certain organizational structures outperform others in terms of adaptability, scalability, and resilience under specific material and informational conditions.

Culture, in this sense, functions as a selectively transmitted system of information, and human historicity is expressed through the accumulation, transmission, and reconfiguration of such structures over time. Failure is not a deviation from progress, but a central epistemic and adaptive mechanism: it is through breakdowns, inefficiencies, and competitive elimination that informational systems improve.

My political philosophy is grounded in scientific rationality and pragmatism, which I regard as the most reliable methods for understanding and managing complex social systems. Politics, from this perspective, is not primarily a domain of moral absolutes, but a problem of adaptive coordination under constraint. Efficiency here is always relative to empirically assessable outcomes: innovation, growth, error-correction capacity, institutional learning, and systemic resilience. For these reasons, I tend to favor decentralized and minimally intrusive institutional arrangements when models and empirical evidence indicate that they maximize adaptive variation and feedback, while recognizing that regulatory structures are sometimes necessary to correct systemic risks, coordination failures, and long-term collective action problems. The role of the state, in this view, is not to impose substantive ends, but to maintain stable background conditions that allow decentralized experimentation, competition, and the development of individual capacities. Excessive centralization tends to reduce the evolutionary bandwidth of social systems, limiting variation and slowing adaptive feedback. I hold qualified sympathies for technocratic decision-making in domains where complexity and technical expertise are indispensable, such as science, health, and environmental management, while rejecting centralized and dogmatic dirigisme or the political imposition of comprehensive social goals that are insulated from empirical revision.

This commitment to institutional adaptability and constrained governance is influenced by the economic work of Milton Friedman, by neoclassical growth models such as those of Solow and McKenzie, and by Schumpeter's theory, as well as by the analyses of Hayek (Carl Schmitt, Barro, Armen Alchian) and von Neumann on decentralized coordination and strategic interaction.

Contemporary authors such as Hans Rosling, Steven Pinker, Paul Bloom, and Daniel Kahneman have also shaped my outlook, alongside European figures including Mario Monti, Mario Draghi, and Romano Prodi. I engage more selectively with the work of Samuelson, Peter Diamond, Leonid Hurwicz, Kenneth Arrow, and especially Robert Lucas, Nancy Stokey, Thomas J. Sargent and Edward Prescott.

Some may classify this orientation as neoliberal, Nozickian, or market-liberal. I do not treat these labels as ideological commitments, but as descriptive shortcuts. My positions are not derived from moral doctrine, but from what I take to be the pragmatic conclusions of economic modeling and empirical analysis, always understood as revisable under improved data and better theoretical tools.

The idea of a negative or constraint-based realism is particularly compelling to me, in which reality is approached primarily through the limits, failures, and corrections it imposes on our models rather than through access to positive essences. In this sense, processes of negotiation between agents, institutions, and models constitute a fundamental mechanism across political, economic, social, and epistemic domains, serving both as a practical coordination tool and as a heuristic for managing systemic uncertainty. Ultimately, my philosophy is an exercise in structural modeling and pragmatic evaluation. I hold that the central questions concerning knowledge, explanation, and social organization can be addressed only through the integration of formal modeling, evolutionary reasoning, and empirical constraint. I admire authors such as Stephen Yablo, Huw Price and Cheryl Misak (and, in different ways, Rorty and Stein) for their deflationary and temporal perspectives, and I remain skeptical toward moralistic or humanistic approaches when they function as substitutes for causal and structural explanation. What matters is not what is declared to be meaningful, but what persists, what adapts, and what works.
